7.7A Differential Association and Social Learning

Sutherland explains how people learn deviance through relationships. Akers extends that account by explaining how imitation and consequences help behavior begin, continue, change, or stop.

Edwin H. Sutherland: deviance is learned

Differential Association Theory rejects the idea that deviant behavior is simply inherited, caused by poverty, or produced by an inherently “bad” personality. Sutherland argued that people learn criminal or deviant behavior through communication with others—especially within close, meaningful groups.

The word differential matters. People encounter different associations that communicate competing messages. A close friend may define cannabis use as ordinary and socially valuable; a parent may define it as unhealthy or illegal; a team may attach consequences to it; an online group may normalize it. The person does not mechanically copy everyone. What is learned depends on the pattern and significance of those contacts.

Why adolescence matters: initiation often occurs while peer networks, identity, independence, and belonging are especially important. Showing adolescents in this theory is descriptive, not an assumption that every adolescent uses drugs or that peer exposure makes use inevitable.

What is actually learned?

Techniques
How to obtain, prepare, administer, conceal, share, combine, or avoid a substance—and sometimes informal harm-reduction practices.
Motives and drives
Reasons use is presented as worthwhile: belonging, experimentation, coping, pleasure, performance, rebellion, ritual, or relief.
Rationalizations
Explanations that make use seem reasonable: “everyone does it,” “this drug is natural,” “I can control it,” or “the rule is unfair.”
Attitudes and definitions
Meanings attached to the behavior: acceptable or unacceptable, admirable or embarrassing, safe or dangerous, normal or deviant.

Sutherland’s nine propositions—in plain language

1Behavior is learnedDrug-related deviance is not treated as an automatic instinct.
2Learning occurs through interactionCommunication may be spoken, modeled, gestured, or symbolically communicated.
3Intimate groups matter mostClose friends, family, partners, and trusted peers generally teach more than impersonal media alone.
4Content includes action and meaningPeople learn techniques as well as motives, rationalizations, attitudes, and definitions.
5Rules are interpretedGroups teach whether laws and norms deserve obedience, resistance, indifference, or selective application.
6Balance of definitions mattersDeviance becomes more likely when definitions favorable to rule violation outweigh definitions unfavorable to it.
7Associations differInfluence varies by frequency, duration, priority, and intensity.
8Learning mechanisms are ordinaryDeviance is learned through the same general processes used to learn conventional behavior.
9Needs do not explain the choiceBoth conventional and deviant behaviors may pursue money, status, relief, or belonging; those needs alone cannot explain which behavior is selected.
The key proposition: “excess” does not mean a literal head count of drug-using versus non-using acquaintances. It refers to the overall weight of definitions as shaped by how often, how long, how early, and how meaningfully they are communicated.

Ronald Akers: Social Learning Theory

Akers and Burgess extended Sutherland by specifying additional learning mechanisms. Association exposes a person to models and definitions; reinforcement helps explain why a behavior is repeated or reduced.

Differential association

Who a person spends time with and the behavioral patterns those groups display.

Definitions

Personal attitudes and meanings that approve, excuse, condemn, or conditionally accept use.

Imitation

Trying behavior after observing a model, especially one who is admired, similar, or rewarded.

Differential reinforcement

The balance of rewards and costs—social and nonsocial, anticipated and experienced—that follows behavior.

Why one experience may increase use while another reduces it

Reward follows use

Pleasant internal effects + peer approval + status or belonging

Probability of similar future use may increase.

⇄

Aversive consequence follows use

Unpleasant effects + disapproval + conflict or loss

Probability of similar future use may decrease.

Important qualification: one unpleasant experience does not guarantee that use stops. A person may expect a different outcome next time, receive approval from another group, change the dose or setting, or continue because relief from withdrawal or distress is powerfully reinforcing.

Sutherland versus Akers

Sutherland asks:

Which groups communicate definitions favorable or unfavorable to deviance, and what do they teach?

Akers adds:

Whom does the person imitate, and which rewards or punishments make the learned behavior more or less likely to recur?

Using the theories in a research paper

A strong application moves beyond writing “peer pressure caused drug use.” Identify the learning process and connect evidence to a specific theoretical concept.

1. Identify associations

Who communicates with the person? How close, frequent, long-lasting, early, or important is that relationship?

2. Identify learned content

What techniques, motives, rationalizations, attitudes, argot, and definitions are communicated?

3. Compare definitions

Which messages favor rule violation, which oppose it, and which make use conditionally acceptable?

4. Add Akers

What is imitated? Which social or internal rewards and costs follow the behavior?

5. Use evidence carefully

Distinguish observed peer influence from a person selecting friends who already resemble them.

6. Avoid determinism

Explain increased or decreased probability—not that association automatically produces use.

Model thesis: “Differential Association Theory helps explain initiation because close peers taught both the technique of use and definitions that framed it as normal and socially valuable; Akers’s Social Learning Theory further explains continuation through the combination of desired effects, group approval, and repeated reinforcement.”

Limitations and evaluation

  • Selection versus socialization: people may choose friends with similar attitudes, so similarity does not prove that peers caused the behavior.
  • Measurement: frequency, duration, priority, intensity, and the “balance” of definitions are difficult to measure precisely.
  • Agency and resistance: people interpret, reject, or selectively accept group messages.
  • Structure: the theory needs help from conflict, strain, neighborhood, policy, and inequality theories to explain why associations and opportunities are distributed differently.
  • Biology and pharmacology: social learning alone does not explain metabolism, tolerance, withdrawal, pain, or drug-specific effects.

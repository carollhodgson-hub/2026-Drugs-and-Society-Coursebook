From Moral Entrepreneur to Drug Specialist

Begin with what you think you know. Build an evidence portfolio across the course. Finish by showing what research confirmed, complicated, or changed.

The purpose

Public drug policy has often been shaped by confident claims made before reliable evidence was available—or by selective evidence presented without context. This project makes that process visible. Your first presentation preserves your starting assumptions about one drug. Each course section then adds a different kind of evidence. Your final paper and presentation replace a one-dimensional warning with a researched sociological analysis.

Part One · Starting claimPresent your initial concern and proposed response as a moral entrepreneur.
Part Two · Research portfolio and paperCollect one focused contribution from every section, then synthesize the evidence in an APA paper.
Part Three · Specialist presentationExplain what changed, present your best-supported conclusions, and recommend a defensible policy.

Part One: Moral Entrepreneur

Choose one drug that interests you. Prepare a brief written statement and first in-class presentation about its dangers to the public and the response you initially believe society should take. Use what you have heard or presently understand, but label these statements as initial beliefs rather than verified facts. The purpose is to document your starting point, not to reward exaggeration.

  1. Name the drug and the danger you currently consider most important.
  2. Identify who or what you think is harmed and whom you want to persuade.
  3. Explain the policy or social response you initially support.
  4. Identify where your impressions came from: family, peers, school, news, entertainment, social media, personal observation, or another source.
  5. Name evidence that might complicate or change your position.
  6. Save the original statement unchanged for comparison at the end of the semester.

Opening question: If your statement became the basis of policy tomorrow, who might benefit, who might be harmed, and what do you still need to know?

Part Two: Build the research portfolio

The prompts below are research checkpoints, not sixteen separate papers. Add each item to one organized project file as you complete the course. Your final paper should synthesize related findings under clear headings rather than mechanically repeating sixteen disconnected paragraphs.

1 · Definition and social constructionPreserve your opening claim, define “drug,” inventory the sources of your beliefs, and identify socially constructed meanings.
2 · HistoryTrace public, medical, and cultural uses over time. Identify important changes in marketing, meaning, or acceptance.
3 · Law and social controlCompare former and current laws, public perception, enforcement, intended outcomes, and latent consequences.
4 · TaxonomyGive the drug class, source plant or chemicals, pharmaceutical and brand names when applicable, slang, routes, and interaction cautions.
5 · Brain and bodyExplain short- and long-term effects, dose, tolerance, dependence, and important individual or contextual differences.
6 · SubculturesAnalyze relevant groups or settings, including norms, language, identity, socialization, access, and protective practices.
7 · Explanatory theoriesApply two theories to initiation, escalation, maintenance, reduction, or recovery. Compare their strengths and limits.
8 · Negative health consequencesDistinguish acute, chronic, overdose, interaction, and social risks without treating every user or pattern as identical.
9 · Drug-using lifestylesExamine how routines, stigma, unsafe supply, legal status, housing, and healthcare access magnify or reduce harm.
10 · InstitutionsAnalyze relevant institutions such as family, healthcare, education, work, the military, law enforcement, or sports.
11 · EconomyConsider healthcare, treatment, enforcement, incarceration, employment, productivity, premature death, markets, and tax effects.
12 · CrimeTest competing causal directions and Goldstein’s pathways. Distinguish drug effects, common causes, market violence, and law-created crime.
13 · Current policyIdentify the present combination of prohibition, decriminalization, legalization, medical regulation, and harm reduction.
14 · Treatment and recoveryCompare evidence-based options, whole-person needs, barriers, continuing support, and a justified improvement.
15 · Education and testingDesign accurate, age-appropriate education. Address family and school roles, peers, privacy, and testing when relevant.
16 · Future policyRecommend a long-term approach, anticipate tradeoffs and unintended effects, and specify how society should evaluate it.

Research paper requirements

  • Length: 6–9 double-spaced pages of body text. The title page and References page are additional.
  • Format: APA 7 style, submitted as a Word document or PDF.
  • Organization: Use meaningful headings and synthesize the section checkpoints into a coherent argument.
  • Minimum sources: This coursebook and at least two additional sources: one authoritative drug-information source and one peer-reviewed academic journal article. A well-supported paper may require more than this minimum.
  • Evidence: Cite factual claims in the text and include every cited source on the References page. Evaluate authorship, date, methods, purpose, and possible limitations.
  • Scope: Include a brief, relevant contribution from every course section, but devote the most space to the evidence that matters most for your drug and argument.

The before-and-after evidence audit

Include a concise comparison in the paper or presentation. Intellectual growth does not require reversing every belief; it requires showing why a conclusion is now better supported and where uncertainty remains.

Opening belief Evidence examined What research showed Revised conclusion Policy consequence
What did I initially claim? Which historical, scientific, sociological, legal, or economic evidence did I use? Was the claim confirmed, qualified, contradicted, or still uncertain? What do I now conclude, and how confident am I? What should society do differently because of this evidence?

Part Three: Drug Specialist presentation

  • Maximum length: 8 minutes.
  • Visual support: Use PowerPoint or another accessible visual presentation. Include images, videos, charts, or data only when they support the argument.
  • Course synthesis: Briefly represent the contribution of all 16 sections; related sections may be grouped.
  • Visible growth: Compare the original moral-entrepreneur claim with the final evidence-based conclusion.
  • Expertise: Explain important evidence in your own words, distinguish association from causation, and acknowledge limits or disagreement.
  • Recommendation: End with a defensible policy, treatment, education, or harm-reduction recommendation and one question that remains unresolved.
  • Citations and access: Cite evidence on slides, include a final References slide, add useful alt text, use readable contrast and type, and caption video whenever available.

Final readiness check

Before submitting, ask: Did I distinguish the effects of the substance from the effects of its social setting and legal status? Did I avoid stereotypes and unsupported causal claims? Did I consider who benefits, who pays, and whose experiences are missing? Can a reader trace my conclusions to credible evidence? Did I explain how my thinking changed?

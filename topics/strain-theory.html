7.7C Social Structure, Anomie, and Strain

Robert K. Merton asked why a society that strongly promotes success may also generate pressure toward deviance when legitimate opportunities remain unequal.

The same cultural goal, unequal starting routes

Achievement route

School → credentials → stable job → income → housing. Every step depends on quality, cost, location, time, health, transportation, networks, and discrimination.

Inherited-advantage route

Property, tuition support, family connections, investment assets, or a direct inheritance can shorten the route. Inheritance is not deviance; it reveals unequal access to resources that make goals easier to reach.

The social structure creates unequal pressure

Social structural theories examine how institutions and patterned inequality contribute to different rates of crime and drug involvement across social locations. They ask why risks and opportunities cluster in particular categories, neighborhoods, occupations, or markets.

Cultural goals

Outcomes society presents as desirable. U.S. examples include wealth, consumption, occupational success, social status, security, and influence.

Question: What does the culture tell people they should achieve?

Institutionalized means

Approved routes to the goals, including education, credentials, stable employment, lawful enterprise, saving, and professional networks.

Question: Who can realistically access those routes?

Strain and anomie are related, but not identical

Strain is pressure produced by the mismatch between valued goals and accessible legitimate means. Anomie is a broader weakening of commitment to institutional rules, especially when a culture emphasizes achieving success more than following approved means. At the individual level, anomie is sometimes described more loosely as normlessness or detachment from conventional expectations.

These are modes of adaptation, not permanent personality types. A person can respond differently across goals, settings, and periods of life.

How the theory connects to drugs

Innovation and drug markets

A person may accept wealth or status but use prohibited means such as selling drugs when legitimate routes appear blocked. This is Merton’s clearest drug-related application.

Retreatism and drug use

Textbooks often use severe, socially withdrawn substance involvement as an example of rejecting both conventional goals and means. This must not define all people who use drugs.

Conformity and legal drugs

A person may pursue conventional success through education and work while using caffeine, alcohol, legal cannabis, or prescribed medication. Drug use alone does not identify an adaptation.

Rebellion and drug policy

A movement may reject existing goals and rules and propose replacements, such as shifting from punishment toward public health, regulated access, or harm reduction.

Analyze the behavior’s function. Selling a drug for income, using one to withdraw from social demands, taking medication to keep working, and campaigning to replace drug laws involve different relationships to goals and means.

Using strain theory in the research paper

1. Identify the goal

What culturally valued outcome mattered?

2. Demonstrate the barrier

What evidence shows unequal access to approved routes?

3. Name the adaptation

Show whether goals and means were accepted, rejected, or replaced.

4. Explain the mechanism

Connect the goal–means gap to pressure and then to a response without claiming inevitability.

Model thesis: “Merton’s strain theory explains participation in the illicit drug market as innovation: the participant accepted financial success but, after encountering restricted access to stable employment and credentials, pursued that goal through institutionally prohibited means.”

Strengths and limitations

  • It shifts attention from individual defect to unequal opportunity and cultural pressure.
  • It explains how deviance may pursue values promoted by the wider society.
  • It emphasizes economic success and explains instrumental behavior better than every form of drug use.
  • It does not fully explain why people facing similar barriers choose different responses.
  • Social learning, control, labeling, conflict, psychological, and pharmacological theories supply missing parts.

7.7D Control and Social Bond Theories

Walter Reckless asks what contains pressures toward deviance. Travis Hirschi asks why people conform and how bonds to conventional society make deviance more costly.

From causes to socially situated choice

Biological theories may emphasize genetic vulnerability, metabolism, receptors, tolerance, or withdrawal. Psychological theories may emphasize unconscious conflict, emotion, personality tendencies, learning, or cognition. These influences can constrain a person’s control without turning behavior into an automatic biological command.

Sociological control theories begin from a different question: if rewards, relief, status, excitement, or opportunity can motivate deviance, why do most people conform much of the time? Their answer focuses on the controls and social bonds that restrain action.

Choice is not the same as equal freedom. Poverty, violence, pain, discrimination, dependence, limited treatment, coercive relationships, and unequal opportunity can narrow the realistic alternatives until one choice feels almost unavoidable. Control theory still treats action as behavior occurring among alternatives, but sociology examines who gets meaningful alternatives, support, and consequences.

Walter Reckless: Containment Theory

Reckless proposed two control systems that work against pushes and pulls toward deviance. Inner containment operates within the person. Outer containment comes from the person’s relationships and social environment. Effective containment does not erase motivation; it buffers or restrains it.

Pushes and pulls

Pushes: frustration, anger, deprivation, family conflict, exclusion, boredom, pain, stress.

Pulls: peer approval, access, excitement, money, status, group membership, expected pleasure or relief.

🧑🏽
Situated actor

interprets pressures, controls, consequences, and alternatives

Two containment systems

Inner: positive self-concept, self-control, goal orientation, frustration tolerance, responsibility, norm commitment.

Outer: supervision, supportive relationships, consistent expectations, meaningful roles, opportunities, limits, and fair consequences.

Strong containment

A teen offered pills at a party may think, “That does not fit who I am,” remember an athletic or academic goal, contact a trusted person, and leave with friends. Motivation or curiosity can exist while controls make use less likely.

Weak or disrupted containment

If the same teen lacks support, sees few attainable goals, expects group status from use, and faces inconsistent consequences, fewer controls oppose the immediate reward. Use becomes more likely—not inevitable.

Travis Hirschi: Social Bond Theory

In Causes of Delinquency (1969), Hirschi developed the control tradition by focusing on bonds connecting people to conventional society. Stronger bonds generally increase reasons to conform and make deviance more socially, emotionally, and practically costly.

❤️

Attachment

Emotional connection to parents, teachers, partners, mentors, or conventional peers. A person considers their expectations because the relationship matters.

Drug example: calling a trusted sibling instead of remaining in a risky setting.

🎯

Commitment

Investment in conventional goals such as education, employment, health, reputation, housing, or family responsibilities.

Drug example: avoiding behavior that could threaten a scholarship, job, license, or valued role.

🧩

Involvement

Time and energy devoted to conventional activities. Involvement structures time and connects people to supervised roles and networks.

Drug example: work, caregiving, rehearsal, or practice reduces unstructured opportunity and supplies alternatives.

⚖️

Belief

Acceptance that conventional rules, laws, and expectations are morally valid or legitimate—not merely fear of punishment.

Drug example: viewing a safety rule as fair increases voluntary compliance; unequal enforcement may weaken legitimacy.

A careful connection: Hirschi belongs to the control tradition, but he did not simply rename Reckless’s inner controls. Reckless distinguishes inner and outer containment against pushes and pulls; Hirschi identifies four social bonds that tie a person to conventional society.

Using control theory in the research paper

Identify motivation and pressure

What reward, relief, opportunity, push, or pull made the behavior attractive?

Identify inner controls

How did self-concept, goals, responsibility, or frustration tolerance restrain—or fail to restrain—the behavior?

Identify outer controls

Which relationships, supervision, roles, opportunities, rules, or consequences were present, absent, inconsistent, or unfair?

Analyze all four bonds

Name the relevant attachment, commitment, involvement, and belief rather than saying only that the person had “weak bonds.”

Explain choice without blame

Describe the alternatives the person actually perceived and whether structural conditions narrowed those alternatives.

State probability, not destiny

Strong controls can reduce likelihood without guaranteeing conformity; weak controls can increase likelihood without causing deviance.

Model thesis: “Containment Theory explains why immediate relief and peer approval pulled the student toward misuse while disrupted family supervision and a weakened academic self-concept reduced restraint. Hirschi’s Social Bond Theory adds that declining attachment, commitment, involvement, and belief lowered the social and personal costs of continued use.”

Limitations

  • Strong bonds may connect a person to a drug-using group rather than conventional society.
  • Heavy involvement is not always protective; some settings contain their own substance-use norms.
  • The theory may understate pharmacological dependence, trauma, mental illness, pain, and structural inequality.
  • Rules perceived as discriminatory or inconsistently enforced may weaken belief in their legitimacy.
  • “Weak bonds” should never be treated as a moral defect or individual diagnosis.

Section 10: Institutional Correlates of Drug Use

Drug patterns do not develop outside society. Families, healthcare, the military, law enforcement, and sport shape access, stress, norms, surveillance, consequences, and routes to help.

Opening story · beyond the “addictive personality”

10.1 Maya Never Stops—She Switches

Maya’s sister says she becomes “addicted to everything.” When Maya stops drinking, she vapes constantly. When she reduces nicotine, online shopping takes over. Later it is gaming, food, exercise, or another activity that promises immediate relief. Her family describes her as having an addictive personality because the object changes while the urgency seems to remain.

The phrase feels explanatory, but it is not an official personality-disorder diagnosis and researchers have not identified one universal personality profile that predicts every addiction. Traits such as impulsivity, sensation seeking, anxiety sensitivity, compulsivity, or difficulty delaying rewards can raise risk for some people and some behaviors. They do not form a single destiny. Genetics, trauma, stress, learning, mental health, pain, opportunity, social networks, product design, dose, and reinforcement also matter.

Immediate reward

A substance or behavior offers relief, stimulation, escape, belonging, or control.

Learning and repetition

The reward strengthens a pathway, while tolerance, cues, withdrawal, or consequences can change the pattern.

Substitution

If the underlying need and environment remain unchanged, another reward may become more attractive—but substitution is not inevitable.

A label can distort decisions. Calling Maya simply “the addictive type” can hide why she uses, which harms are present, and which social supports would make recovery more likely.

10.2 Institutions organize opportunity, risk, and response

An institutional correlate is a patterned relationship between drug use and an organized part of society. Correlation does not prove that an institution or occupation caused an individual’s use. Institutions distribute medications and alcohol, produce distinctive stresses, define acceptable conduct, create peer cultures, monitor behavior, and decide whether disclosure leads to care or punishment.

FamilyMedical personnelMilitaryLaw enforcementSport
Keep four categories separate: prescribed therapeutic use, nonmedical use or misuse, a diagnosable substance use disorder, and impairment while performing a safety-sensitive role. One does not automatically establish another.

10.3 Family: risk, protection, and shared consequences

Families transmit norms and coping practices, regulate access, supervise young people, and provide attachment and material support. They can also be sources of conflict, trauma, stress, or exposure. Genetic vulnerability may be shared within families, but genes operate through development and environment rather than determining a fixed outcome.

When one member’s use affects the family

Effects may include financial strain, missed work or school, disrupted caregiving, fear, conflict, legal costs, housing instability, stigma, and health burdens. Children may take on adult responsibilities; partners may become monitors or crisis managers. Families can also develop resilience through treatment, peer support, safety planning, and redistribution of roles. A life-course analysis asks how family conditions shaped use and how use later reorganized family life.

10.4 Medical personnel: access, strain, and professional identity

Medical personnel are not one uniform group. Patterns differ by occupation, specialty, setting, schedule, age, and substance. Relevant conditions include rotating shifts, sleep loss, burnout, pain, traumatic exposure, medication access, knowledge that may encourage self-treatment, and fear that disclosure will damage a license or career. Access may affect which substances are available; it does not by itself explain why use begins or becomes harmful.

The identity of “healer” can encourage recognition of risk, yet make admitting illness feel incompatible with competence. Confidential Physician Health Programs coordinate evaluation, treatment, continuing care, and monitoring. Early help can protect clinician and patient; discipline remains necessary when safety is compromised, but fear of automatic punishment can delay disclosure.

Safety without stigma: distinguish having a treatable disorder from being impaired at work. Effective systems secure medications, monitor diversion, provide protected routes to care, and use evidence-based return-to-practice decisions.

10.5 Military: readiness, unit culture, and reintegration

Random testing and strict rules can suppress and detect illicit drug use, while legal alcohol may remain embedded in recreation or bonding. The 2018 Health Related Behaviors Survey estimated past-year illicit drug use among active-duty members at 1.3% and prescription-drug misuse at 1.4%; self-report, testing, and diagnostic records measure different populations and behaviors. Current military surveillance continues to track alcohol- and substance-related diagnoses because they affect health and readiness.

Important pathways include injury and pain treatment, deployment stress, trauma symptoms, sleep disruption, separation from family, reintegration, unit climate, and concern about command or career consequences. These conditions do not describe every service member. They show why prevention must address leadership norms and confidential care—not only individual discipline.

10.6 Law enforcement: legal alcohol inside an enforcement culture

Research does not justify treating all officers or departments as having one alcohol pattern. It does identify recurring conditions that can increase risk: traumatic events, post-traumatic stress symptoms, disrupted sleep and rotating shifts, hypervigilance, burnout, a culture of self-reliance, alcohol-centered peer bonding, and fear that help-seeking could affect fitness-for-duty decisions or promotion. In one study, PTSD symptoms—not exposure alone—were associated with alcohol and drug use, illustrating the importance of the intervening mechanism.

The institution also creates a role conflict: officers enforce drug and impaired-driving laws while alcohol remains legal and culturally available. Protective responses include trauma-informed supervision, confidential counseling, peer support with clear privacy limits, scheduling reform, early screening, and treatment pathways that protect the public without equating help-seeking with unfitness.

10.7 Sport: four patterns with different purposes

Doping

Use of a prohibited substance or method to enhance performance or evade detection. It raises health, fairness, coercion, and consent questions.

Restorative use

Medication or treatment intended to manage pain, inflammation, sleep, injury, or recovery. It may be appropriate, but pressure to return can mask injury or prolong dependence.

Additive use

Substances used to add strength, muscle, endurance, alertness, or weight change. Products range from ordinary nutrition to prohibited drugs.

Recreational use

Alcohol, cannabis, nicotine, or other substances used for pleasure or social belonging. Team norms, season, stress, and testing policies still shape the pattern.

The World Anti-Doping Agency’s 2026 Prohibited List specifies substances and methods banned at all times, in competition, or in particular sports. A Therapeutic Use Exemption can permit an otherwise prohibited treatment when medical criteria are met. The NCAA’s 2023 survey reported declines in binge drinking and narcotic pain-medication use but growth in daily nicotine vaping, reminding us that patterns change. Education must include prescribed and over-the-counter medicine, supplement contamination, recovery pressure, mental health, and informed consent—not simply a list of banned products.

10.8 The Big Three: putting the institutions together

Structural functionalism

Institutions coordinate care, readiness, public safety, family support, and fair competition. Ask when testing, licensing, discipline, or treatment performs those functions—and when stigma, secrecy, or role strain becomes a dysfunction.

Conflict theory

Ask who defines acceptable use, who is tested, who can obtain confidential treatment, whose career is threatened, and who profits. Institutional actors do not possess equal power.

Symbolic interactionism

The same substance may be labeled “medicine,” “coping,” “doping,” “bonding,” or “deviance.” Labels can shape identity and whether people disclose use.

Section 12: Drugs and Crime

Do drugs cause crime, does crime create opportunities for drug use, or do laws help produce the relationship we observe?

Opening story · Firsthand account

12.1 Cody and Siobhan: when punishment becomes part of the harm

Content note: This account discusses arrest, substance-use problems, financial and employment loss, and suicide. Cody and Siobhan are pseudonyms. The events occurred in North Carolina, which imposes an unauthorized-substances tax in addition to criminal penalties.

In 1999, Cody purchased pills from Taiwan. After the package went to the wrong person, the sheriff’s department arranged for it to be delivered. Officers also found approximately half an ounce of marijuana, paraphernalia, and a dead marijuana plant in a pot.

Siobhan did not know about the pills, but her name was on the lease. Both roommates faced charges. Because she could not afford separate counsel, Cody hired one attorney to represent them both. She later wondered whether independent representation would have protected her interests better. The case ended with pleas involving marijuana possession and paraphernalia and required community service.

Financial and institutional consequences

An initial unauthorized-substances-tax assessment emptied their shared bank account—including Siobhan’s student-loan refund. In this account, the initial weight included the dead plant and soil. Some money was returned much later after laboratory measurement, but the disruption had already occurred. Because North Carolina has returned much of this tax revenue to the investigating agency, the arrangement raises a conflict-theory question about institutional incentives.

Criminalization
Arrest, charges, legal expense, tax assessment, labels, and service.
→
Collateral consequences
Lost educational security, blocked work, stigma, untreated needs, and strain.

Cody lost a juvenile-court advocacy job he wanted. His employment instability and substance-use problems increased, but the court outcome did not provide rehabilitation. He later died by suicide. This account does not claim the case alone caused his death; suicide has complex causes. It asks how punishment, blocked opportunity, untreated needs, and hopelessness can accumulate.

Strain
A record can block legitimate opportunities.
Labeling
A “drug offender” label can narrow identity and opportunity.
Control
Weakening work, education, and supportive bonds may reduce informal control.

If this story raises personal concerns, call or text 988 in the United States.

12.2 Association is not one causal arrow

Justice-involved populations report drug use at higher rates, but that association cannot tell us what came first or why. A stronger explanation asks about initiation, escalation, and maintenance across a life course.

Key correction: No model explains every person, drug, or offense. Alcohol has the clearest population-level connection with violence, but its effects still depend on dose, person, expectations, setting, and context.

12.3 Goldstein’s drug-use and prostitution study

Paul J. Goldstein’s 1979 exploratory study interviewed a convenience-quota sample of 60 women who used drugs. Forty-three reported prostitution experience and 17 did not. Because every participant used drugs and the sample was small and nonrandom, it cannot estimate prevalence among all sex workers or prove causation.

43 reported prostitution experience 17 other drug-using participants

Four relationships identified

Economic
For some, costly dependence came first, and earnings or direct exchange helped obtain drugs.
Psychoactive or coping
For some, drug use followed entry into prostitution and helped cope with danger, stigma, or distress.
Functional
In some settings, a substance was used to endure hours, approach clients, or manage an interaction.
Subcultural
Drug use and prostitution could be learned and reinforced in overlapping networks of access, norms, work, and relationships.

Language and ethics: The original research used labels and class rankings that are reproduced only when describing its categories. Contemporary discussion should distinguish consensual adult sex work from trafficking, coercion, and sexual exploitation.

12.4 Goldstein’s three pathways to drug-related violence

The tripartite framework does not claim all drug use is violent. It classifies the mechanism in a particular incident, and pathways may overlap.

PsychopharmacologicalDrug effects may contribute to impaired judgment, fear, agitation, or disinhibition in a particular person and context.
Economic-compulsiveIncome-generating crime may finance expensive compulsive use; violence is often incidental rather than a desired drug effect.
SystemicViolence arises from illegal-market territory, debt, retaliation, robbery, hierarchy, product disputes, and punishment of informants.
OverlapAn intoxicated participant in a market debt dispute may fit several models. Classification needs event-level evidence, not assumptions from a drug test.

12.5 Summary chart: theories of drugs and crime

Explanation Direction or mechanism What it asks Key caution
Psychopharmacological Drug effects → event Did a specific dose, person, and setting affect judgment or aggression? A positive test does not establish motive or causation.
Economic-compulsive Cost/dependence → income crime Was the offense committed to finance costly compulsive use? Not every drug is costly, not every user is dependent, and most users do not commit predatory crime.
Systemic / illegal-market Market rules → crime Did territory, debt, retaliation, or lack of lawful dispute resolution produce violence? This concerns market organization, not only chemistry.
Crime-precedes-drugs Delinquent network → use Did offending or illegal-market participation begin first? Sequences vary; “crime first” is not universal.
Common-cause / problem behavior Shared conditions → both Do trauma, strain, exclusion, opportunity, impulsivity, or peers explain both? A “spurious” correlation redirects the causal question; it does not erase either outcome.
Subcultural / differential association Learned norms ↔ both How do networks teach definitions favorable to use, selling, or offending? Association is not destiny, and subcultures are diverse.
Strain, labeling, and social control Blocked bonds → recurring harm Do stigma, records, lost work or school, and weakened bonds narrow lawful opportunities? These explain reaction and life-course consequences, not every initial act.
Reciprocal / life-course Drug use ↔ crime over time How do initiation, escalation, and maintenance change across turning points? The dominant pathway can change for one person.
Best question: Which drug, which offense, which person, which social context, which law or market, and at what point in the life course?

12.6 Drug-facilitated crime: incapacitation, consent, and coercive control

Content note: This subsection discusses sexual assault, trafficking, kidnapping, robbery, involuntary intoxication, and memory loss.

Drug-facilitated crime occurs when intoxication or impairment is used to commit or conceal another offense. In sexual assault, two pathways are especially important: an offender may deliberately administer or pressure a person to consume a substance, or may exploit a person who became impaired voluntarily. Consent must be freely given and ongoing; intoxication never transfers responsibility to the victim.

Correct the popular image: alcohol is the substance most commonly detected in drug-facilitated sexual-assault investigations. Rohypnol, GHB, ketamine, and other sedatives matter, but focusing only on a stereotypical “date-rape drug” can obscure alcohol, medications, multiple substances, and opportunistic offending.

Why toxicology cannot answer every question

Timing matters
Some substances leave blood or urine quickly. A negative result does not prove that no substance was involved.
Detection is not causation
A positive result does not by itself establish dose, timing, secret administration, consent, or the offender’s intent.
Memory may be fragmented
Impaired memory is compatible with victimization; it should not be used to discredit a report.
Context completes the evidence
Messages, witnesses, drink containers, video, medical findings, and the survivor’s account may all matter.

Drugs within a criminal enterprise

The same mechanisms can further robbery, kidnapping, trafficking, and forced criminal activity. An enterprise may use intoxication to subdue a person, build a drug debt, create or exploit dependence, threaten withdrawal, impair memory, or make a victim appear less credible. These are techniques of power and control—not proof that every person who uses drugs is involved in crime.

If this may have happened: seek urgent medical care and a forensic examination as soon as possible. If safe, preserve a drink container, clothing, messages, and other possible evidence. In the United States, contact RAINN at 800-656-HOPE (4673), use its online chat, or call 911 for immediate danger. A delayed report or negative toxicology result does not make the survivor responsible.
